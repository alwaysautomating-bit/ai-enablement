# Visual Identity Discrimination Skill

Every product has a visual identity — even if it was never intentionally designed. The identity is the sum of its anchors: the typographic choices, the structural weight, the palette logic, the spatial rhythm, the specific way elements make contact with each other.

When a design feels wrong but you can't say why, it's usually because the wrong identity's anchors are present. The skill is identifying which anchors belong to this product and which belong somewhere else.

---

## The Identity Anchor System

An identity is held in place by a small number of anchors — the elements so specific that they define what a product looks like even when everything else is stripped away.

Identify the anchors before designing, not after. If you can't name them, you'll borrow them from somewhere else without realizing it.

### Anchor Categories

**Typographic anchors**
- Weight: does this identity use heavy type, light type, or mixed?
- Structure: serif, sans-serif, monospace, or hybrid?
- Role of type: decorative, structural, or purely functional?
- Scale relationship: how does large type relate to small type?

**Structural anchors**
- Does this identity use hard edges or soft edges?
- Is whitespace a design element or a gap?
- Does it use borders, dividers, and lines — or implied separation?
- Is it grid-tight or loosely composed?

**Palette logic**
- Is color used sparingly (one accent) or generously?
- Is the palette warm or cool?
- Does color carry meaning (red = alert, green = safe) or is it purely aesthetic?
- Is dark mode the default or the exception?

**Textural presence**
- Does this identity use noise, grain, or texture?
- Is it smooth and digital or does it have a physical-world reference?
- Does it feel printed or rendered?

## Drift Detection

Identity drift happens when elements from one identity appear in another. It is almost always invisible until someone points it out — and then it cannot be unseen.

### The "Missing Eyeliner" Signal
When a design feels like it's trying to be the right thing but something is missing — a specific sharpness, a defining element — that absence is the drift signal. The design has the structure but not the signature.

The inverse is also true: when a design has elements that feel foreign — too soft where it should be hard, too textural where it should be clean — those foreign elements are the drift.

**Ask:** What does this design have that it shouldn't? What does it not have that it should?

### Common Drift Patterns

**Texture bleeding**: A product with a physical, aged, or textural identity (vintage aesthetic, bookplate style) bleeds its texture into a product that should be clean and digital. The result looks "off" without an obvious reason.

**Weight confusion**: A heavy, bold-first identity (like a typographic enforcement product) bleeds its visual weight into a light, airy consumer product. Or vice versa — a structural product loses its visual authority by adopting the softness of a lifestyle brand.

**Color contamination**: The accent color from one product appears as a secondary element in another. Palette associations transfer whether you intend them to or not.

**Tone mismatch**: A playful, consumer-warm typographic style appears in an interface that should read as professional and enforcement-first. The users feel it even if they can't articulate it.

## Correction Protocol

When drift is identified:

### Step 1: Name the anchors of this product
Write down, specifically, what this product's visual identity is anchored by. Not adjectives — specific elements.

Example: *"Bold black typographic elements at structural positions. No texture. Dark background with a single cool accent. Monospace type for data. Generous whitespace. Hard edges."*

### Step 2: Audit the current design against the anchors
For each element in the design, ask: does this belong to this product's anchor set, or to another identity?

Elements that don't belong are removed, not modified.

### Step 3: Identify the contamination source
Where did the foreign elements come from? Usually one of:
- A product you were just working on before switching to this one
- A design reference that was the right aesthetic for a different product
- A default choice (a font, a texture, a layout pattern) that belongs to another product in the portfolio

Identifying the source prevents the same drift from recurring.

### Step 4: Restore the anchors
Add back the elements that are missing. Remove the elements that don't belong.

This is not redesign. It is calibration — returning to the identity that was established, not inventing a new one.

## Design Identity Questions (Use Before Starting Any New Product Design)

1. What is the single most important visual anchor for this product?
2. What products am I currently working on that could bleed into this one?
3. What does this product's identity specifically exclude? (What must it never look like?)
4. If I could only keep one visual element and everything else disappeared, what would I keep?
5. What is the "eyeliner" — the specific element whose absence would make the design feel like an impersonator?
