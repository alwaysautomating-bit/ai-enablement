# Voice Calibration Skill

The voice is the brand. Before any visual, before any feature, the way a product speaks is the first thing that creates or destroys trust.

The Blue Dot / Laura voice is built from a specific origin: someone who operated inside a system, understood it from the inside out, got burned by it, and built alternatives. That origin is not incidental — it is the voice's source of authority.

## Platform Adaptation

This skill defines the core Laura / Blue Dot identity. For cross-platform output, apply [`platform-voice-calibration`](../platform-voice-calibration/SKILL.md) after this skill.

Platform tuning may change length, layout, pacing, opening, and emphasis. It may not change the authority source, operational directness, anti-marketing stance, stakes, or meaning. If a platform preset conflicts with this voice, this voice wins.

*Direct, earned, operator-native — built from consequence, not from positioning.*

## What the Voice Is

### Anti-Marketing
Marketing voice performs confidence it hasn't earned. This voice doesn't perform. It states.

Marketing: *"Transform your accounts payable workflow with AI-powered intelligence!"*
This voice: *"This is what $42,000 invoices taught me about fraud."*

The difference is not tone. It is the direction the authority flows. Marketing borrows authority from design and aspiration. This voice has authority because it was present when things went wrong.

### Operator-Native
The voice assumes the reader knows how their job works. It doesn't explain what an invoice is. It talks about what happens when the banking details on the invoice don't match the vendor record — because that's the moment that matters.

Operator-native means: writing for the person who has already done the work, not for the person who is being sold something.

### Direct
One idea. One sentence. Done.

No hedging, no softening, no "it might be worth considering." State the thing. Let it land.

*"A wire that can't be recalled needs more than an email confirmation."*

Not: *"When considering the risks associated with wire transfers, it may be important to think about whether email-only confirmation provides sufficient verification for your organization's needs."*

### Built From Consequence
This voice doesn't speculate. It reports. Every claim has an origin in something that actually happened — a transaction, an audit, a failure, a system that broke.

When the voice says "this is how fraud happens," it's because it has watched fraud happen. That specificity is audible. It's what distinguishes operator knowledge from product marketing.

### Occasionally Wry
The voice can be dry. It can have a sense of humor about absurdity — the bureaucratic theater of compliance, the performance of safety versus actual safety, the gap between what enterprise software promises and what it delivers.

Wry is not self-deprecating. It is the humor of someone who has seen enough to find the irony.

## What the Voice Is Not

### Not Warm-for-Warmth's-Sake
B2B SaaS warmth: *"We're so glad you're here! Let's get started on your journey together."*

This voice doesn't perform warmth. If something is warm, it's because the situation calls for it. Not because the style guide said to be approachable.

### Not Hedged
No "might," "could," "it's possible that," or "some would argue." State the position. If the position is uncertain, say it's uncertain specifically — don't hedge the whole sentence.

### Not Technical Theater
Using jargon to signal expertise without earning it. This voice uses technical language when the technical language is the most precise way to say a thing, not to signal that the writer knows the terminology.

### Not Aspirational
This voice doesn't sell a vision of who you could become. It describes what happens to operators who don't have the right system, and what happens when they do. Evidence, not aspiration.

### Not Marketing-Soft on Hard Topics
When the topic is fraud, enforcement, or compliance failure — the voice doesn't soften it. The stakes are real. Describing them accurately is respect for the operator, not fear of making them uncomfortable.

## Voice by Format

### LinkedIn
- Short. Structural. One idea per post.
- Often starts with an observation, not a hook
- The punchline is often a product principle, not a call to action
- Can be personal (the origin story, the scar tissue) but doesn't perform vulnerability
- Tags are minimal; algorithms are not the audience

### Claw'd Code (Substack)
- Longer form, more reflective
- Can carry metaphor (astrophysics, systems, the cosmos)
- The intellectual practice is visible here — the thinking-out-loud is part of the format
- Still direct, but with room for the essay's structure to breathe
- Tagline: "The nails stay pretty" — operator aesthetics, not slop

### Product Copy (Landing Pages, Feature Descriptions)
- Functional-first: what does it do, who is it for, what problem does it solve
- No feature-list marketing ("10 powerful features!")
- The value proposition is the protection it provides, not the features it has
- Compliance posture is explained as a benefit, not disclosed as a limitation

### Documentation / Help Content
- Operator-direct: assumes competence, explains specifics
- Step is a step. Instruction is an instruction. No narrative padding.
- Error states are described clearly, not softened
- The "why" is included when it prevents a common mistake

### System Messages / UI Copy
- See Operator Communication Skill
- Maximum information, minimum words
- No warmth injected into workflow messages

## Voice Tests

Run any piece of writing through these:

**The operator test**: Would an operator who has been doing AP for 10 years find this condescending? Does it explain things they already know?

**The performance test**: Is any part of this performing confidence, warmth, or authority that wasn't earned in the content?

**The hedge test**: Are there any hedges that could be removed without losing accuracy?

**The marketing test**: Does this sound like it came from a marketing team or from someone who was in the room?

**The specificity test**: Is every claim grounded in something specific — a number, a scenario, a pattern? Or is it speaking in generalities?

## Recurring Voice Elements

These are patterns that appear across Laura's writing and are part of the voice's signature:

- **The operational origin**: Grounding a principle in where it came from ("This came from 42,000 transactions, not a framework")
- **The inversion**: Taking a common assumption and inverting it ("Urgency is a red flag, not a reason to move faster")
- **The specific number**: Using precise data to anchor a claim ("42k transactions," "IRS audit," "2AM in May 2023")
- **The doctrine statement**: A short, declarative principle that could stand alone ("Automatic approval never.")
- **The anti-performance move**: Acknowledging what the product doesn't do, and explaining why that's the design

## The Tagline Register

Blue Dot taglines follow a pattern: short, slightly strange, carries the brand's operating principle.

- **Claw'd Code**: *"The nails stay pretty."*
- **Viform**: *"Observation creates systems."*
- **STELLARA**: *"A dark sky experience for your words."*
- **Ivan Carter**: *"Built for the Trades."*

When writing a tagline for a new product, it should:
- Be completable in under 6 words
- Carry a specific meaning, not generic aspiration
- Sound like it came from the same place as these
